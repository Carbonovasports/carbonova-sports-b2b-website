How Much Does a Private Label Pickleball Paddle Cost?

The honest answer is "it depends" — but on what, exactly? This guide breaks down every cost driver in a private label paddle so you can read a quotation, compare suppliers and brief your factory with the right information.

Every brand owner asks the same question before the first quotation: what does a private label pickleball paddle cost? The useful version of that question is not a single number — it is a map of the decisions that change the number. Face material, core, construction, finish, packaging and quantity each move the unit price, and most of them are choices you control.

This guide walks through each cost driver in the order a factory thinks about them, explains the direction each one pushes the price, and ends with the information you need to get a quotation that actually reflects your product.

The six cost drivers behind every paddle

A factory prices a paddle as the sum of its specification, not as a flat product. Six decisions drive the unit price:

  • Face material — the grade and weave of the surface layer.
  • Core — honeycomb or foam, and the thickness.
  • Construction — thermoformed or flat lay-up, and the shape.
  • Surface finish — raw texture or printed graphics.
  • Components — grip, edge guard and handle.
  • Packaging — bulk, retail box or premium kit.

Quantity sits on top of all six because it determines how the fixed costs — tooling, set-up, printing plates — spread across units.

Face material: the largest variable

Face material is usually the biggest single cost driver, and the spread between entry and premium faces is wide. The key principle: cost rises with the grade of the material and the density of the weave.

  • Fiberglass — the entry-level face; the lowest material cost in the range and the softest response.
  • Carbon fiber weaves — cost rises from 3K through 12K to 18K weave densities, and premium grades such as T700 raw carbon sit at the top of the carbon spectrum.
  • Kevlar (aramid) — a woven aramid face priced for its abrasion resistance and hardness; positioned at the premium end alongside top carbon.
  • Titanium texture — a durable textured face with a distinct surface treatment cost.

When two factories quote the same platform at different prices, the first thing to check is which material grade and weave each quote assumes. A "carbon paddle" at one price and a "T700 raw carbon paddle" at another are not the same product.

Core and construction: honeycomb vs foam, flat vs thermoformed

The core and the way the paddle is assembled change the cost in two directions:

  • Core type. PP honeycomb is the volume standard — well understood, efficient to produce and priced accordingly. Foam cores (such as the GEN5 platform) add a denser material and a different process, moving the paddle toward the premium tier.
  • Construction. A flat lay-up is the simpler, lower-cost process. Thermoforming — fusing the face, edge and core under heat and pressure — adds process time and equipment cost but is the construction behind premium performance and durability claims.
  • Thickness and shape. Thicker cores (16 mm is the common performance thickness) use more material. Standard shapes cost less than exclusive geometries that need new tooling.

This is why a "premium feel" story costs more than a "stable and light" story: the engineering underneath is more expensive, and the retail price should reflect that.

Surface finish and branding

What the paddle looks like adds a layer of cost that scales with complexity:

  • Raw texture finish — the cheapest option and often the most marketable, since raw carbon and Kevlar weaves are a premium visual in themselves.
  • UV printing and water transfer — full-colour graphics with set-up and per-unit costs that rise with colour count and print area.
  • Grip and edge guard — custom grip colours, cushioned grips and branded edge guards each carry small but real premiums.

Most first-time brands over-invest in graphics and under-invest in packaging. A clean raw-carbon face with strong retail packaging usually sells better and costs less than a heavily printed face with plain packaging.

Packaging: the quiet cost line

Packaging is frequently the difference between two similar quotations. The range is wide:

  • Bulk packing — paddles in poly bags, carton-level export packing; the lowest packaging cost.
  • Retail-ready box — a printed sleeve or box that can sit on a shelf; a meaningful per-unit addition.
  • Premium kit — box, hang tag, grip tape, insert cards and barcode stickers; the highest packaging tier.

Packaging also has its own minimum print runs, separate from the paddle MOQ. Confirm both before sampling so the sample cost reflects the final retail configuration.

How MOQ changes the picture

Quantity determines which tier of the cost curve you buy at:

  • Pilot quantities (50–100 pcs) — the highest per-unit cost; this is the price of validating a product, and it should be budgeted as development, not as a comparison point for volume pricing.
  • Mid volumes — fixed costs such as tooling and printing start to amortise, and the unit price falls noticeably.
  • Container volumes — the flattest part of the curve; price differences between suppliers narrow because material cost dominates.

The practical rule: never compare a pilot quotation with a volume quotation from another factory — you will be comparing different products on the same line. Ask every supplier for a tiered quote covering both your first order and your scale-up order.

Why "what does a paddle cost" is the wrong question

A paddle is quoted, not priced. Beyond the specification, the quotation reflects:

  • Certification support — designs that need USA Pickleball testing or CE documentation carry coordination effort.
  • Quality control — weight and balance checks, in-process and pre-shipment inspection add cost that prevents bad batches.
  • Sampling — the 2–4 day sampling turnaround and iteration rounds are part of the service, and they are worth paying for.
  • Shipping terms — EXW, FOB, CIF or DDP change your landed cost by the freight and risk allocation, not by the paddle.

A slightly higher unit price from a factory with documented quality systems and certification experience is usually cheaper than a lower price from a supplier that cannot back the product. Our OEM sourcing guide explains how to weigh these factors.

Getting a quote that reflects your product

Factories quote fastest and most accurately when the enquiry is complete. Send these with your request:

  • Target market and player segment
  • Platform preference or reference paddle
  • Face material and core (or ask for options)
  • Expected quantity and launch timeline
  • Branding and packaging requirements
  • Destination port and shipping terms

With those details, a factory can produce a tiered quotation and a sampling plan in one round — which is faster and more useful than a generic "please quote me paddles".

One more habit worth adopting: send the same complete enquiry to two or three factories and compare the itemised breakdowns, not the bottom lines. Two factories quoting the same spec will still differ on material grade assumptions, inspection depth and packaging inclusions — and those differences are exactly where the real cost of the product is decided.

Hidden costs beyond the unit price

The quoted unit price is not the full cost of landing a paddle on your shelf. Budget these lines separately:

  • Freight and duties — the shipping term (EXW, FOB, CIF, DDP) decides who carries freight and risk; import duties and taxes are a separate calculation per destination.
  • Sample shipping — samples are usually shipped express and paid for by the buyer; factor in several rounds during development.
  • Third-party inspection — if your quality plan includes independent pre-shipment inspection, that service has its own fee.
  • Warehousing and fulfilment — landed paddles need storage and pick-pack labour before they reach customers.
  • Certification coordination — USA Pickleball testing and CE documentation involve laboratory fees and time, even when the factory coordinates the process.

A realistic landed-cost model includes all of these. Comparing only unit prices is how brands discover "the cheaper supplier" was the expensive one by the time freight and inspection were added.

Cost vs quality: where not to cut

Some cost levers are safe, and some are traps:

  • Safe to cut: packaging complexity, number of variants, printed graphics area, courier speed on non-critical documents.
  • Risky to cut: material grade, core quality, process control, inspection depth — these determine the feel, weight consistency and defect rate your customers will judge.

A paddle is a product of process as much as materials: two factories quoting the same spec can deliver different products because press parameters, inspection discipline and BOM control differ. When a quotation looks too good, ask what changed — the answer is usually material grade or inspection depth, and both become your problem after the container lands.

The same principle applies to sampling: a sample built by hand in a development room costs less to make than a production batch, so confirm that the quoted price reflects production conditions, not a one-off prototype.

Budget for the paddle, then build the story

The cost of a private label paddle is a range, not a number — and the range is defined by choices you make deliberately: the material story, the construction, the packaging and the volume tier. Budget a pilot first, learn from sell-through data, and let the reorder unlock the volume curve.
